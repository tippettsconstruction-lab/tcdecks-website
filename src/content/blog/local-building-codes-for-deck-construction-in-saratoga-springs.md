Saratoga Springs is home base for TC Decks, and we've been through the city's permit process many times. Building codes can feel like red tape, but most of the rules exist because decks fail in predictable ways: loose ledger connections, undersized footings, and railings that give way. Here's a plain overview of what to expect. The specifics change over time and differ from city to city, so treat this as a starting point. The Saratoga Springs building department has the final word.

## Do you need a permit?

In most cases, yes. As a general rule, a deck more than 30 inches above the ground needs a permit and guardrails. Decks attached to the house usually need one too, because the connection to the house carries a lot of load. A small, freestanding deck close to the ground may not, but check with the city before you assume that.

Covered decks and pergolas add roof framing and sometimes electrical work, and those usually go through review as well.

## Utah uses the International Residential Code

Utah adopts the International Residential Code (IRC) with state amendments, and cities like Saratoga Springs, Lehi and Eagle Mountain enforce it locally. That code covers decks in detail: footing size and depth, beam and joist spans, ledger attachment, guard height, stair rise and run, and more. Your city's building department sets which edition and local amendments apply, and it can change.

## What the city reviews

A typical deck permit application includes a site plan showing where the deck sits on the lot, and drawings showing the framing, footings, ledger attachment, stairs and railing. The plan reviewer checks that:

- **Footings** are sized for the load and go deep enough to stay below the frost line.
- **Beams and joists** are sized and spaced correctly for the span and the decking material.
- **The ledger** is properly flashed and fastened to the house, or the deck is designed to be freestanding.
- **Guards and stairs** meet height, opening and rise-and-run rules.
- **Setbacks** keep the deck the required distance from property lines and easements.

If you live in a neighborhood with an HOA, the HOA may have its own approval process for materials, colors and location. That's separate from the city permit.

## Snow load

Utah County gets real snow, and decks have to be designed for it. Required snow loads vary by location and elevation, so the number that applies to a lot near Utah Lake may differ from one up on a bench or in a canyon. Your city's building department sets the requirement. A covered deck or pergola roof has to carry that snow, too.

## Inspections

Expect several inspections during the build. They usually include:

1. **Footings**, before concrete is poured or once ground screws are installed.
2. **Framing**, before the deck boards cover the joists, beams and connections.
3. **Final**, once the railings, stairs and everything else are done.

Each inspection has to pass before the next stage can be covered up. That's a good thing. It means someone besides the builder has looked at the parts of the deck you'll never see again.

## Railings and stairs

These are the details homeowners notice most, and the ones inspectors check closely. Guards are required along open sides above a certain height, with limits on how big the openings can be so a small child can't slip through. Stairs need consistent rise and run, graspable handrails and adequate lighting. ## Why unpermitted decks cause problems

An unpermitted deck can come up when you sell your house, when you file an insurance claim, or when a neighbor complains. Fixing it after the fact can mean opening up finished work so it can be inspected. It's much easier to do it right the first time.
